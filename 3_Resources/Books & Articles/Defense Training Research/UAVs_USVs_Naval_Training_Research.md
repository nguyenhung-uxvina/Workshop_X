# UAVs and USVs in Modern Naval Training and Exercises

## Platform landscape for naval UAVs and USVs

Uncrewed aerial vehicles (UAVs) and uncrewed surface vessels (USVs)—often grouped more broadly as “unmanned/uncrewed vehicles (UVs)”—have moved from niche enablers to mainstream components of modern naval operations and training. Contemporary naval programs emphasize that these systems can be remotely operated, semi-autonomous, or (with sufficient maturity) increasingly autonomous, and can carry sensors, weapons, or other mission payloads. citeturn9view0turn22view0

A widely used way to explain why navies pursue uncrewed systems is the “dull, dirty, dangerous” (3D) framing: missions that (a) require long endurance and monotonous presence, (b) occur in hazardous environments (mines, chemical/CBRN risk areas, heavy electronic warfare), or (c) expose humans to capture or casualty risk—especially in contested littorals. A recent Congressional summary describes these advantages directly for naval uncrewed systems, emphasizing suitability for long-duration and high-risk missions and noting the cost/space advantages of designs that do not need to support onboard crews. citeturn9view0turn22view0

In naval contexts, the “platform landscape” is typically best understood as **families of systems** rather than a single drone type. A practical taxonomy is:

- **Naval UAVs**, spanning long-range maritime patrol/ISR aircraft to ship-launched tactical drones and shipborne vertical takeoff-and-landing (VTOL) UAVs. The same rotational logic applies across sizes: persistent sensing, cueing, and communications support to surface and air forces. citeturn20view0turn20view1turn20view3  
- **Naval USVs**, ranging from small craft for point surveillance, mine countermeasures, and force protection through medium/large craft intended to extend a fleet’s sensing and strike capacity—often via modular payloads and manned-unmanned teaming. citeturn22view0turn19view4turn9view1  

From a naval modernization standpoint, a key theme is “distributed” employment: spreading sensors and weapons across more nodes to complicate adversary targeting and sustain combat power even if individual platforms are lost. A U.S. congressional summary notes that large USVs are intended to operate alongside crewed ships, serving as adjunct weapon magazines and/or sensor platforms to support distributed concepts of operations. citeturn22view0

## Key UAV and USV categories used in modern navies

### UAV categories most relevant to naval exercises

**High-altitude, long-endurance maritime ISR (HALE).** These systems are built for wide-area surveillance and persistent maritime intelligence collection. A representative example is the MQ‑4C, described in official Navy material as an autonomously operated system providing persistent maritime ISR, integrated with broader maritime patrol and reconnaissance “family of systems” constructs. citeturn20view3

**Shipborne VTOL ISR UAVs.** VTOL drones are attractive in naval training because they can launch and recover from ships without runways, and they can provide “over-the-horizon” sensing for ships operating dispersed. The MQ‑8C is presented in official Navy facts as a ship-operable system with substantial payload capacity and multi-hour endurance, aligning with the role of shipborne ISR extension. citeturn20view1

**Ship-launched or expeditionary small tactical UAVs.** These systems trade payload for accessibility and rapid deployment. Official Navy material on the RQ‑21A explicitly frames it as providing day/night ISR, target acquisition, and communications relay—features that map directly onto exercise needs (ISR lanes, kill-chain rehearsal, and networking under contested conditions). citeturn20view0

**Close-range shipboard ISR services (small fixed-wing).** For ship-based training, rail-launched fixed-wing UAVs enable long loiter with relatively small footprint. Navy fact material describes systems like ScanEagle as ship-usable, EO/IR-equipped ISR platforms with 20+ hour endurance, often contractor-operated under ISR services constructs. citeturn20view2

### USV categories most relevant to naval exercises

**Small and medium USVs for experimentation, force protection, and payload carriage.** Large-scale exercises increasingly use USVs as real-world testbeds to “carry payloads,” gather intelligence, and collect data on operational integration. A Naval Sea Systems Command review of a major exercise highlights the milestone of running multiple USV prototypes in parallel and using them to carry payloads and provide intelligence while operating alongside other participants. citeturn19view0

**Medium-to-large USV prototypes integrated with fleets and allies.** Official Navy documentation describes “Overlord” USVs as designed to conduct naval warfare operations independently or with crewed surface combatants, and notes their integration with fleet events, including deployments and multinational activity in the Indo-Pacific region. It also highlights that the Navy introduced a “Modular Surface Attack Craft” concept intended to merge medium and large USV capabilities into a streamlined platform for flexibility and cost considerations. citeturn19view4

**Large USVs as distributed magazines/sensors and modular payload carriers.** A recent congressional report explains that large USVs are meant to deploy directly from pier (rather than from the deck of a crewed warship) and be equipped with weapons and/or sensors to operate in conjunction with crewed ships as part of distributed operating concepts. It further documents that the Navy reorganized large-USV acquisition by merging prior program lines into a Modular Attack Surface Craft program, explicitly emphasizing modular payloads and mission areas such as ISR/targeting support and information operations. citeturn22view0turn9view1

## Tactical roles in exercises: ISR, communications relay, and long-range targeting

Modern naval training uses UAVs and USVs to rehearse *the full sensor-to-decision-to-effect chain*, not just “flying a drone.” The most consistently emphasized roles in exercise design align closely with your requested categories: **ISR**, **communications relay**, and **target designation/cueing for long-range fires**.

**ISR and maritime domain awareness.** Naval UAVs provide layered surveillance: wide-area maritime scan (HALE ISR), shipborne ISR extension (VTOL), and tactical close-in identification (small fixed-wing or quadcopter-like systems, depending on navy). Official descriptions of the RQ‑21A highlight day/night ISR, actionable intelligence delivery, and a modular payload approach including AIS receivers and (in some configurations) radar and signals intelligence growth paths. citeturn20view0  
On the USV side, large exercises use USVs as moving sensor nodes—collecting data on how payloads behave in realistic sea conditions and how information is fused into a broader operational picture. citeturn19view0turn22view0

**Communications relay and networking under dispersion.** In a dispersed fleet, the “value” of sensing depends on moving data into the command-and-control (C2) fabric fast enough to matter. Official Navy facts explicitly list “communication relay” as a core mission function for the RQ‑21A, reflecting its use as an airborne node that extends the reach of line-of-sight networks for units at sea or operating ashore in the littorals. citeturn20view0  
At the doctrinal level, distributed fleet concepts emphasize resilient communication links and networking technologies to knit widely dispersed manned and unmanned ships and aircraft into a coordinated force, explicitly anticipating attempts to degrade communications and networks. citeturn24view0turn13view0

**Target designation and cueing for long-range fires.** Exercises increasingly treat UAVs (and sometimes USVs) as *kill-chain sensors*, not only ISR collectors. A concrete example comes from a major multinational naval exercise in which an unmanned aerial vehicle provided aerial targeting information and visual confirmation so a common unmanned surface vessel could fire guided rockets at a target; the UAV also performed battle damage assessment. This is a direct, publicly documented instance of UAV-enabled targeting for long-range (or at least standoff) fires mediated through an unmanned surface platform. citeturn19view1  
This aligns with broader joint C2 approaches that frame modern operations as “sense,” “make sense,” and “act” across domains and with partners—emphasizing data fusion, decision speed, and resilient information sharing as prerequisites for timely fires. citeturn12view0turn13view0

## Using UAVs and USVs to simulate opposing forces and asymmetric threats

Exercises do not only use unmanned systems as “friendly assets”; they increasingly use them as **opposing-force (OPFOR) emulation tools** to replicate how an adversary might fight with low-cost, attritable, or swarm-capable systems. This includes both **swarming** behaviors (many small nodes saturating sensing and defense) and broader **asymmetric threats** (explosive drone boats, one-way attack craft, deception, and mixed-domain harassment).

Two real-world trendlines have strongly shaped how navies think about OPFOR emulation:

1) **Combat-proven maritime drone attacks in the Black Sea.** Open-source professional analysis describes uncrewed “naval drones” as having sunk and damaged major warships, presenting a credible model of how relatively small systems can impose strategic costs on larger fleets. citeturn25view1turn25view2  
2) **The spread of explosive drone boats and similar systems to other theaters.** Reporting on attacks against commercial shipping notes the growing operational and insurance risk implications, and explicitly ties the perceived effectiveness of Black Sea drone attacks to threat perceptions elsewhere. citeturn25view0  

Against that backdrop, exercise designers increasingly use unmanned systems in at least three OPFOR-relevant ways:

**Swarm-event rehearsal (force protection and infrastructure defense).** A U.S. naval report on an Allied experimentation event describes a live demonstration in which two groups of unmanned surface vessels simulated a swarming attack, while defenders deployed USVs in response to disrupt attackers and protect critical infrastructure. This is an unusually explicit public example of **swarm threat simulation** as a training objective. citeturn18view5

**Counter-unmanned tactics development (defend-as-you-integrate).** NATO experimentation reporting emphasizes that exercises are not only about employing unmanned assets but also about developing tactics, techniques, and procedures to defend against unmanned threats—recognizing that widespread adoption creates a parallel requirement for counter-swarm and counter-drone proficiency. citeturn18view3

**Electromagnetic spectrum and deception stress-testing.** Dedicated events are being run to evaluate how unmanned systems behave as carriers for sensing, electronic attack, communications, and deception tools. For example, a U.S. Navy-led “Silent Swarm” event is publicly described as experimentation with technologies employable on small multi-domain unmanned systems, potentially including surface craft and unmanned airborne systems. citeturn19view5  
Separately, the Navy has publicly demonstrated swarm mission-planning software intended to optimize groups of unmanned aircraft (and other unmanned configurations) through simulation, reflecting the institutionalization of swarm-scale thinking in planning and experimentation. citeturn23view0

## Unmanned systems as connectors for joint and combined maritime operations

In modern maritime operations, the most strategic value of UAVs and USVs often comes from their role as **network nodes** that connect sensors, shooters, and decision-makers across services and coalition partners. This is not only a technical integration problem; it is a training and doctrine problem.

A central driver is the need to maintain operational advantage in an environment where adversaries seek to impede or deny command-and-control capabilities. A joint C2 strategy summary emphasizes that adversaries increasingly target C2 and that joint approaches aim to achieve “sense, make sense, and act” at speed across domains and with partners—supported by secure and resilient infrastructure and improved information sharing. citeturn12view0turn13view0

Naval doctrine and planning documents align with this networking emphasis. A distributed maritime operations primer notes that distributed concepts depend on resilient communications and networking to integrate dispersed manned and unmanned forces, explicitly acknowledging the challenge of operating when networks degrade under attack. citeturn24view0  
In parallel, the Department of the Navy’s unmanned campaign framing highlights the need for robust networks and integrated human-machine teaming across the fleet, with the campaign spanning doctrine, organization, training, leadership/education, and policy as well as materiel solutions. citeturn16view0turn16view1

Operationally, multinational exercises are already being used as **integration laboratories**:

- A Pacific Fleet experimentation report describes a multi-domain unmanned exercise in which USVs integrated with allied navies and marine expeditionary forces to advance manned-unmanned teaming and improve decision speed and lethality—explicitly naming that the point is confidence-building and operational integration across the force. citeturn19view2  
- NATO exercise reporting highlights the use of secure networks (including 5G mesh approaches in trials) and C4 elements to enable interoperability, tasking, and information exchange between maritime unmanned systems across the underwater, surface, and air realms. citeturn26view0turn18view3  

In other words, UAVs and USVs are increasingly trained not as “standalone platforms,” but as parts of **kill webs**: distributed sensing and decision nodes that allow multiple services (surface, air, coastal fires, and maritime special operations elements) to coordinate effects with less dependence on a single large platform.

## Benefits, constraints, and technical gaps that exercises surface

### Benefits consistently demonstrated in training and experimentation

**Reduced risk to personnel.** A live-fire exercise report explicitly links unmanned vehicle use to keeping sailors “out of harm’s way,” articulating a direct training rationale for shifting tasks to USVs/UAVs where possible. citeturn19view1  
At the broader program level, congressional reporting frames uncrewed systems as especially suitable for high-risk missions where injury, death, or capture is a concern. citeturn9view0turn22view0

**Persistence and operational tempo.** Platform facts routinely show endurance levels that are well-suited to “persistent presence” training lanes (maritime surveillance, convoy screening, choke point monitoring). Examples include 24+ hour endurance for HALE maritime UAVs and 20+ hour endurance for small fixed-wing shipboard UAVs, enabling exercise designers to run longer, more realistic ISR timelines than many crewed aviation options can sustain at similar cost. citeturn20view3turn20view2

**Cost and scalability advantages—paired with modular payload logic.** A congressional summary emphasizes that uncrewed systems can be individually less expensive than crewed ships and aircraft in part because they do not need crew-support spaces and equipment. citeturn9view0turn22view0  
The newest large-USV program framing also emphasizes modular payloads (containerized weapons and/or sensors), a design approach that is closely aligned with scalable training and rapid mission reconfiguration in exercises. citeturn9view1turn22view0

### Technical and organizational constraints repeatedly highlighted

**Autonomy maturity and safety/reliability at sea.** Congressional reporting identifies reliability of propulsion for transoceanic voyages and the maturity of autonomy technology as recurring oversight issues for large USV programs. citeturn22view0turn9view1

**Digital infrastructure, C2 integration, and “data plumbing.”** A major audit report warns that early investments risk underperforming if the supporting information technology and digital infrastructure do not keep pace. It highlights gaps in portfolio management, progress measurement, and prototype evaluation criteria—pointing to the reality that scaling unmanned operations is as much a systems engineering and governance problem as a platform problem. citeturn11view1turn11view3

**Cybersecurity, regulations, and limits of autonomous decision-making.** A recent peer-reviewed review of market-ready USVs notes that regulatory constraints, cybersecurity risks, and limitations in autonomous decision-making remain persistent challenges, even as autonomy, propulsion, endurance, and communications technologies improve. citeturn28view0

**Communications fragility and jamming pressure in real conflict environments.** Reporting on maritime drone warfare notes that remote operation and satellite links can expose systems to jamming and other countermeasures, and highlights the continuous action–reaction cycle in which navies must adapt systems and tactics. citeturn25view3turn13view0

## Future trends and strategic importance for doctrine and readiness at sea

Three strategic trends stand out across official doctrine framing, acquisition reporting, and exercise design.

First, unmanned systems are being treated as **doctrinally integral** to distributed maritime operations and contested littoral concepts, not “add-ons.” Official campaign framing explicitly aligns unmanned vision to distributed operations and emphasizes that success requires a holistic approach across doctrine, training, personnel, and policy—so that unmanned technologies operate inside a broader networked warfighting architecture. citeturn16view0turn16view1turn22view0

Second, the trajectory is toward **modular, scalable, and coalition-interoperable** unmanned forces. Congressional reporting documents the consolidation of prior large-USV program lines into a modular concept (MASC), describing a future USV defined by reconfigurable mission capability via modular payloads with initial mission areas including ISR/targeting support and information operations—exactly the roles exercises need to stress in distributed kill-chain training. citeturn9view1turn22view0  
An official Navy fact file similarly points to streamlined platform concepts intended to merge medium and large USV capabilities for operational flexibility and lower cost, reinforcing that “modularity + experimentation” are moving together. citeturn19view4

Third, the next phase of naval readiness is likely to be shaped by **swarm-scale thinking** and rapid iteration. Public Navy reporting on swarm mission planning software underscores how simulation and large numbers of runs are being used to optimize multi-vehicle arrangements and payload mixes, reflecting a shift from “single-drone employment” to “team-of-systems employment” as a standard planning and training problem. citeturn23view0  
NATO experimentation reporting mirrors that direction by emphasizing multi-domain experiments, secure communications, and combined trials to accelerate doctrine and interoperability—while also explicitly working the defense side of the equation (countering unmanned threats and protecting critical infrastructure). citeturn18view0turn18view3turn18view5

Taken together, these trends suggest that integrating UAVs and USVs into naval training is no longer primarily about proving the platform can operate. It is about proving the **fleet can fight as a network**: distributing sensing and fires, sustaining C2 under pressure, and adapting quickly as adversaries adopt the same tools. citeturn24view0turn13view0turn11view3
